# TU-CIS-1051-Final-Project
Final project of CIS 1051 at Temple University

Video presentation found on YouTube here: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Y7ugtt1R8pA

This project aims to take a political survey of about 5000 respondents and 60 survey questions and make a prediction based on the answers to that survey whether that respondent voted for Donald Trump or Kamala Harris in the 2024 Presidential Election. It will make a prediction and compare it to one of the survey questions (VOTEGEN_POST) that asks whether the respondent voted for Donald Trump or Kamala Harris. The intended goal of this project is to take several machine learning models that will make predictions versus the actual survey response. 

The first step was to find an appropriate political database to have fun with. This project started with an extensive search of political databases on the internet. Some databases were from years in the past, too small, did not have relevant political questions, were not conducted by a reputable source, were not in an Excel csv file, or were blocked behind a paywall. Eventually, I found a database that was recent, large, relevant, from a reputable source, in a file that I could use to make meaningful comparisons, and free. ​The survey I chose was the 2025 National Public Opinion Research Survey from
the Pew Research Center. This survey asks a key survey question: "Who did you vote for in the 2024 Presidential election?" (labeled VOTEGEN_POST in the survey), and asks dozens of other survey responses that could be analyzed to make a machine learning prediction. Having found a usable database, it is time to execute the goal of this project.​​

In order to figure out which survey responses would be most effective at being features that are used to make predictions, I created 55 PivotTables in Excel in order to determine highly variable relationships between reponses of survey questions. PivotTables can summarize and analyze large datasets very easily, showing comparisons, patterns, and trends about the data. Each PivotTable used one survey question and crossed it with VOTEGEN_POST, the survey feature this project aims to predict. This was repeated for every relevant survey question (for example, INTERVIEW_DATE was not compared in a PivotTable because it doesn't have any meaningful data, but UNITY, which is an actual survey question, was), which totaled to 55 survey questions compared in PivotTables crossed with VOTEGEN_POST. The PivotTables that showed the most relevant variability were chosen to use to predict whether someone voted for Donald Trump or Kamala Harris in the 2024 Presidential election. In the end, 13 survey responses were used as inputs features to the machine learning models. While there was a highly correlated relationship between the various political party affilitation survey questions and VOTEGEN_POST, I decided to leave these survey questions out of my prediction model because they are too correlated. The prediction would not be very meaningful because one could simply look at their response for PARTYSUM and make a very accurate prediction. Undoubtedly, adding party affiliation would increase the models' accuracy, but we don't learn as much about the other survey features. For example, knowing whether a respondent was in the Democratic Party can almost certainly predict whether they voted for Kamala Harris. This isn't very insteresting and we don't learn how other factors can affect voting behavior. This project aims to use other relationships to learn about how people vote, not just party affiliation. Initially, I tried to use the CORREL function in Excel to try and make a prediction, but given that the data is categorical and not linear, the CORREL results were not very helpful. CORREL must use quantitative data, and this data set is completely categorical. However, the PivotTables were very helpful in showing the relationships between all of these survey responses. 

After figuring out which survey responses would be effective machine learning features, I dropped the irrelevant VOTEGEN_POST responses of "3", which means the respondent voted for "Another Candidate", and "99" which means "Refused/Left Blank". I did this to focus the prediction models on just the two frontrunner candidates. Once I had my parsed dataframe, I used three different models in order to make predictions: Support Vector Machine, K Nearest Keighbors, and Random Forest. Support Vector Machine models try to find the hyperplane that best separates two classes by maximizing the margin between the represented data points by a linear equation. This margin is the distance from the hyperplane to the nearest support vectors on each side. K Nearest Neighbor models identify the closest data points to an input and makes predictions based on the average value of the data points. KNN assigns categories based on the majority of nearby points, working by proximity and majority to make predictions. Random Forest models creates decision trees to make predictions. Each tree analyzes random parts of the data and combines them together for regression and predictions. The final prediction is the majority vote of each tree. Each method was fairly accurate (above 75%), but RF model was most accurate at an average of 87.5% accuracy. RF can handle non-linear data very well. It scales well for large datasets and can handle outliers easily. RF doesn't care about the scaling of data. For these reasons, RF would naturally perform better than SVM and KNN.

After the models made their predictions, I graphed the results. In the code is a bar graph comparing the three models and their accuracy. It shows that Random Forest is better than K Nearest Neighbor which is better than Support Vector Machine. Next, I created simulated pie charts than can be used to see the differences between the models and the actual response. The actual response showed that 53.83% of voters voted for Kamala Harris and 46.17% voted for Donald Trump. SVM predicted that 55.41% voted for Kamala Harris (-1.58% difference) and 44.59% voted for Donald Trump (1.58% difference). KNN predicted that 57.91% voted for Kamala Harris (-4.08% difference) and 42.09% voted for Donald Trump (4.08% difference). RF predicted that 54.67% voted for Kamala Harris (-0.84% difference) and 45.33% voted for Donald Trump (0.84% difference). Curiously, while KNN had a higher accuracy in the prediction model bar graph (~2% difference in favor of KNN), SVM had a lower difference in the predicted pie chart (~2.5% difference in favor of SVM). RF was most accurate in both the bar graph (87.5%) and pie chart (0.84% difference) comparisons.

The use of this project could be very helpful for a political campaign. Simply by knowing these data points, a campaign can use a machine learning model to prioritize certain voters who are predicted in voting one way or another. Typically, political campaigns focus on voters they know will show up to vote and who are on the fence and need convincing. Sending canvassers to talk to voters is a highly effective way of influencing them to vote for candidates. Political campaigns have limited time and resources, so knowing how someone is likely to vote is incredibly useful information on whether the campaign prioritizes communications with that voter. For example, the Kamala Harris campaign should not prioritize a highly likely Trump voter, because that is a waste of time and money, since they will never convince them otherwise. The Harris campaign would be better served communicating with highly likely Democratic voters who they could hire or have volunteer on behalf of the campaign. Lastly, the ever-important middle ground of undecided voters are the top prize for a campaign. If the campaign can influence enough swing-voters to vote for their candidate, then that could potentially swing the entire election. A machine learning model with a 87.5% accuracy rating is pretty good enough to make an informed decision on which voters should be prioritized. Predicting past voter behavior is a good indication on future voting behavior. Data mining survey respondants could be useful in future elections. If the survey response is large enough, statistical analysis could be used to create confidence intervals about the population as a whole.
