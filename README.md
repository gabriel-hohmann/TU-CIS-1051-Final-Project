# TU-CIS-1051-Final-Project
Final project of CIS 1051 at Temple University

This project aims to take a political survey of about 5000 respondants and 60 survey questions and make a prediction based on the answers to that survey whether that respondant voted for Donald Trump or Kamala Harris in the 2024 Presidential Election. It will make a prediction and compare it to one of the survey questions that asks whether the respondant voted for Donald Trump or Kamala Harris. The intended goal of this project is to take several machine learning models that will make predictions versus the actual survey response. 

In order to figure out which survey responses would be most effective at being features that are used to make predictions, I created 55 PivotTables in Excel in order to determine highly variable relationships between reponses of survey questions. PivotTables can summarize and analyze large datasets very easily, showing comparisons, patterns, and trends about the data. Each PivotTable used one survey question was crossed it with VOTEGEN_POST, the survey feature this project aims to predict. This was repeated for every relevant survey question (LANGUAGE was not used, UNITY was), which totaled to 55 questions. The PivotTables that showed the highest variability were chosen to use to predict whether someone voted for Donald Trump or Kamala Harris in the 2024 Presidential election. In the end, 14 survey responses were used to input at features to the machine learning models. While there was a highly correlated relationship between the various political party affilitation and predicting who a respondant voted for, I decided to leave those out of my prediction model because they are too correlated. The prediction would not be very meaningful because one could simply look at their response for PARTY and make a nearly perfect prediction. This isn't very insteresting and nothing of substance could be learned from using PARTY data. Initially, I tried to use the CORREL function in Excel to try and make a prediction, but given that the data is not linear, the correlation results were not very helpful. However, the PivotTables were very helpful in showing the relationships between all of these survey responses.

After figuring out which survey responses would be effective machine learning features, I used three different models in order to make predictions: Support Vector Machine, K Nearest Keighbors, and Random Forest. Support Vector Machine models try to find the hyperplane that best separates two classes by maximizing the margin between the represented by a linear equation. This margin is the distance from the hyperplane to the nearest support vectors on each side. K Nearest Neighbor models identify the closest data points to an input and makes predictions based on the average value of the data points. KNN assigns categories based on the majority of nearby points, working by proximity and majority to make predictions. Random Forest models creates decision trees to make predictions. Each tree analyzes random parts of the data and combines them together for regression and predictions. The final prediction is the majority vote of each tree. Each method was fairly accurate (above 75%), but RF model was most accurate at an average of 87.5% accuracy. RF can handle non-linear data very well. It scales well for large datasets and can handle outliers easily. RF doesn't care about the scaling of data. For these reasons, RF would naturally perform better than SVM and KNN.

After the models made their predictions, I graphed the results. In the code is a bar graph comparing the three models and their efficacy. It shows that Random Forest is better than K Nearest Neighbor which is better than Support Vector Machine. Next, I created simulated pie charts than can be used to see the differences between the models and the actual response. The actual response showed that 53.83% of voters voted for Kamala Harris and 46.17% voted for Donald Trump. SVM predicted that 55.41% voted for Kamala Harris (-1.58% difference) and 44.59% voted for Donald Trump (1.58% difference). KNN predicted that 57.91% voted for Kamala Harris (-4.08% difference) and 42.09% voted for Donald Trump (4.08% difference). RF predicted that 54.67% voted for Kamala Harris (-0.84% difference) and 45.33% voted for Donald Trump (0.84% difference). Curiously, while KNN had a higher accuracy in the prediction model bar graph, SVM had a lower difference in the predicted pie chart. RF was most accurate in both the bar graph and pie chart comparisons. 

The use of this project could be very helpful for a political campaign. Simply by knowing these data points, a campaign can use a machine learning model to prioritize certain voters who are predicted in voting one way or another. Typically, political campaigns focus on voters they know will show up to vote and who are on the fence and need convincing. Sending canvassers to talk to voters is a highly effective way of influencing them to vote for candidates. Political campaigns have limited time and resources, so knowing how someone is likely to vote is incredibly useful information on whether the campaign prioritizes communications with that voter. For example, the Kamala Harris campaign should not prioritize a highly likely Trump voter, becasue that is a waste of time and money, since they will never convince them otherwise. The Harris campaign would be better served communicating with highly likely Democratic voters who they could hire or have volunteer on behalf of the campaign. Lastly, the ever-important middle ground of undecided voters are the top prize for a campaign. If the campaign can influence enough swing-voters to vote for their candidate, then that could potentially swing the entire election. A machine learning model with a 88.5% accuracy rating is pretty good enough to make an informed decision on which voters should be prioritized. Predicting past voter behavior is a good indication on future voting behavior. Data mining survey respondants could be useful in future elections. If the survey response is large enough, statistical analysis could be used to create confidence intervals about the population as a whole.
